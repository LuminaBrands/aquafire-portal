Installer field practice — framing, sealing & downdraft prevention
==================================================================

Source: Lumina Brands (Stefan), Aug 2026 — distilled from a conversation
with Brad, senior installer. This is field practice from install crews; it
sits alongside, not instead of, the published Specs & Installation
Guidelines (guide-aquafire-*-specs-install-2026.txt). Where the two differ
the difference is called out below.

Published in the portal as the "Installer Field Notes" section of
enclosure-guide.html.

DECISION (Stefan, Aug 2026): the cutout recommendation below was adopted as
the portal's own figure, not just as a tip. MODELS in app.js and builder.js
now carry nominal + 3/8" widths, so the Enclosure Guide, its Quick Reference
table and the Fireplace Builder all report 1/8" wider than the 2026 spec
guides in this folder. That divergence from the published + 1/4" is
intentional; the guide shows the published minimum alongside its own number
so a spec-sheet reader can see why the two differ.


FRAMING THE CUTOUT

  - Recommendation: frame the cutout WIDTH at nominal + 3/8" rather than
    the published nominal + 1/4". For a 60" unit that is 60 3/8" instead
    of 60 1/4" — 1/8" more room overall.
  - Depth and height are unchanged. The published figures are
    W = nominal + 1/4", D = 12 1/4" (Lite 9 5/8"), H = 14" min. (Pro),
    12" (Original), 11" (Lite).
  - Reasons given:
      * Proper fit — the unit seats without being forced into a tight
        enclosure.
      * Airflow — extra space around the internal components helps
        prevent heat build-up and extends unit life.
      * Component safety — a buffer so light strips, fans and other
        delicate internals are not compressed or damaged while the unit
        is being framed in and seated.
  - Brad's summary: being slightly generous with these dimensions rather
    than holding a tight fit reduces installation-related service calls
    and component failures.
  - AMBIGUITY, unresolved: Brad noted the extra clearance "may total 3/4"
    in some contexts depending on how it's measured across the unit" —
    i.e. it is not always clear whether a drawing calls out 3/8" overall
    or 3/8" per side. Confirm which convention a drawing uses before
    cutting. The portal states the recommendation as +3/8" OVERALL width
    and flags the per-side question.
  - Ganged runs (Stefan, Aug 2026): customers commonly buy two inserts —
    80" as two 40s, 100" as a 60 + 40, 120" as two 60s (the Pro and
    Original only; page-compare-vs-aquafire.txt records the Lite as not
    gangable). The units butt together into one continuous ribbon, so the
    portal treats a run as a single opening and applies the same + 3/8"
    once, to the total: 80 3/8", 100 3/8", 120 3/8". Depth and height are
    the single-insert figures. The published guides list the ganged sizes
    under "Other Common Dimensions" as unit dimensions only (80" x 12" x
    8 3/4" etc.) and give no ganged cutout row, so the cutout figure is
    the portal's, derived the same way as the singles.
  - Not to be over-applied: the insert is suspended in the opening from
    its 1/2"-thick flanges (published guides: "Cutout dimensions are
    critical!"), so the generous figure is a small, deliberate margin,
    not licence to open the cutout up further.


DOWNDRAFT PREVENTION — SEALING

  Ideal scenario (unchanged from the published guides, which "strongly
  recommend sealing any open wall cavities within the enclosure to
  prevent downdrafts"):
  - Work with the builder to frame the opening properly and drywall and
    seal the entire bottom enclosure.

  Fallback — the tape method:
  - Trigger: the installer finds an open framing scenario, or feels air
    movement around the enclosure. Common in high-rise condos and in
    homes with "open plenum" HVAC systems.
  - Procedure: flip the unit to expose the underside and wrap the front
    and back edges with 3"-wide black high-quality tape (Gorilla tape or
    equivalent). This seals the unit against air movement that would
    otherwise trigger downdrafts.
  - Timing: do it during the initial install, while the enclosure is
    open — not after symptoms appear. Sealing later means pulling the
    installed unit back out.
  - Rationale: air and moisture infiltration degrades light strips, fans
    and sensors over time. Brad calls the tape a "band-aid" that prevents
    costly service calls.
  - Tape spec matters: 3" wide, high quality, so it covers the front and
    back edges properly.

  DP (downdraft prevention) kit:
  - Where a unit calls for a DP kit, the kit must be sealed properly
    against the surrounding structure. Failing to seal it in high-rise
    condos — where the wall assemblies "breathe" — has caused major
    installation failures.


VENTING — DO NOT SEAL THE UNIT OFF FROM ITS AIR

  - Sealing is about the enclosure cavity, not the intake. Published
    requirement: at least 50 square inches of air intake within the
    enclosure per 20" of unit length, through the top lid or, if the lid
    is covered, a toe kick beneath the enclosure.
  - Brad's caution: installers should check the client is not placing
    heavy items on, or otherwise blocking, the top of the unit. This
    matters more once the enclosure has been fully sealed.


HVAC REALITY IN MODERN CONSTRUCTION

  - Many current homes are built with "open plenum" positive-pressure
    HVAC systems. That engineering trend routinely causes air-pressure
    issues in fireplace enclosures, which is what makes proactive
    sealing critical rather than optional in new construction.


BUILDER / CUSTOMER COMMUNICATION

  - Set expectations up front: present sealing as a proactive best
    practice, not a repair. It prevents future maintenance — replacing
    light strips or fans — which saves the builder time and preserves
    the unit's longevity.
  - The "band-aid" philosophy: do not wait for issues to arise. If the
    enclosure is open, seal it during the initial install so the unit
    does not have to be removed later for repairs.
